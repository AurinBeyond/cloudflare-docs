# Substack / Medium Essay Angles

**Purpose:** Three essay drafts (titles + opening paragraphs + structural
sketch) that fit Anna's "aristocratic quiet" voice and can drive organic
leads to `prulesoul.site/polarstar` without sounding like content
marketing.

**Locked principles:**
- No "5 ways to..." or "How I..." headlines.
- No founder cosplay ("As a founder, I learned...").
- Speak from the felt experience first, then the observation, then the
  link — never the other way round.
- Each essay should be readable in under 6 minutes. Long enough to
  earn the reader's time. Short enough that they finish.

---

## Essay #1 — *"The bedtime story problem nobody is writing about"*

**Compass register:** West · Connection
**Posting venue:** Substack first, Medium re-post 7 days later
**Target length:** ~1,200 words

### Opening paragraph (draft)

> The last time I read my child a bedtime story I did not write
> myself, I noticed something I have not been able to un-notice since.
> The story was fine. Pleasant, even. It rhymed. It had a small
> resolution at the end. My child fell asleep at roughly the same
> rate they fall asleep on any other Tuesday. And yet, closing the
> book, I had the distinct feeling that something had happened in the
> room that I would not have chosen, given the choice. Something
> slightly synthetic. Something assembled.

### Structural sketch

1. **The noticing** — open with the felt moment above. No analysis yet.
2. **The pattern** — describe how most children's media has drifted
   toward the same texture: bright, resolving, faintly didactic, AI-
   adjacent even when not literally AI. No specific brands named.
3. **The hypothesis** — children do not need closure at bedtime; they
   need to be safely accompanied through *un-closure*. The current
   market is optimised for the opposite.
4. **The small experiment** — what happened when I wrote five
   deliberately quiet stories and read them to my own child.
5. **The result** — soft. Not "they fell asleep faster". Something
   subtler. The child started asking for one specific story by name,
   and the asking-by-name was the only metric I trusted.
6. **The link** — single line at the end:
   *"If you would like to read them, they are here:
   prulesoul.site/polarstar."*

### Why this works

- Opens inside a domestic moment, not inside a thesis.
- Names a real anomaly most parents have felt but not articulated.
- Does not attack AI directly — circles it without naming it. Maintains
  the aristocratic quiet tone.
- Link is at the bottom, single line, no CTA dressing.

## Essay #2 — *"What I removed from the children's book I almost wrote"*

**Compass register:** North · Clarity
**Posting venue:** Medium first (Medium rewards "lessons learned" framing
better than Substack), then Substack re-post.
**Target length:** ~900 words

### Opening paragraph (draft)

> I started writing a children's book in early autumn. By the time I
> stopped, the book was twenty-eight pages long and I disliked nineteen
> of them. The pages I disliked were not poorly written. They were
> doing things I had been taught children's books are supposed to do —
> resolve, instruct, comfort by reassurance — and they were doing
> those things competently. I disliked them because, taken together,
> they sounded like a book somebody else had written. I wanted to know
> what was left if I removed everything I had inherited from the
> category and only kept what was actually mine.

### Structural sketch

1. **Opening confession** — the 19 pages I cut, and why.
2. **What gets cut** — the specific moves I removed: the moral, the
   rhyming couplets, the explicit emotional resolution, the named
   adult who solves the child's problem.
3. **What stayed** — what was left after the cuts. Five short stories,
   no morals, no resolutions. A child notices wind. A small object is
   lost and stays lost.
4. **The reaction from one early reader** — a friend who read the
   collection and said it "made her uncomfortable in a way she liked."
   Use this quote verbatim if friend grants permission.
5. **The pricing question** — brief, honest. Why €9 and not free, why
   €9 and not €19. The 90-second version of the same conversation
   from `psp_pitch.md`.
6. **The link** — same single-line, end-only treatment as essay #1.

### Why this works

- Medium's audience is well-trained on "what I removed" framings, and
  the headline survives Medium SEO better than a felt-experience title.
- Doubles as a piece of founder writing that the user can quote on a
  future "About" page, or include in a press inquiry pack.

## Essay #3 — *"On the friend who notices first"* (highest emotional weight)

**Compass register:** North · Clarity + West · Connection
**Posting venue:** Substack only. This one is too emotionally specific
for Medium's SEO economy.
**Target length:** ~1,500 words

### Opening paragraph (draft)

> Sometime in your mid-thirties there is a particular kind of friend
> who notices something is wrong before you do. They do not say it the
> first time. The first time, they make a small adjustment — the
> dinner is moved to a quieter restaurant, the message arrives at a
> better hour, the question is asked with one fewer assumption than
> usual. You feel it, vaguely. You write it off as their good mood.
> The second time, you understand that the adjustments are about you
> and have been for a while, and that the friend was already living
> through the version of the situation you are about to live through
> next.

### Structural sketch

1. **The opening passage above** — sit inside the felt moment for
   ~250 words. No analysis.
2. **The observation** — most relief in adult life comes not from
   solving anything, but from being noticed early by someone who has
   already lived through the thing you are about to live through.
3. **The product implication** — this is what the upcoming "Letters to
   the Woman Who Accepts Too Little" audio collection is. Not therapy.
   Not advice. The friend who noticed first, lent to you by audio.
4. **The honest disclosure** — the product does not exist yet. The
   essay is the announcement that it is coming. The reader who
   recognises themselves in paragraph one is the exact reader the
   product is being made for.
5. **The waiting list invitation** — single line at the end.
   *"If you would like to know when these letters are available,
   the waiting list is at prulesoul.site/whispers (or the equivalent
   site URL by then)."*

### Why this works

- This is the essay that does the heaviest emotional work and
  therefore the one that has to land most precisely. It is the
  pre-launch signal for the Month 2 NORTH-direction product.
- It also functions as Anna's strongest writing sample. Even if it
  drives zero waitlist signups, it works as the "About the writer"
  link for every future product.
- Substack's emotional-content economy rewards this kind of essay far
  better than Medium's "actionable takeaway" economy.

## Publishing cadence

```
Week 1: Little Star audio release + free preview page
Week 4: Essay #1 (West · Connection)          → drives PDF sales
Week 6: Essay #2 (North · Clarity)            → drives PDF sales + founder credibility
Week 9: Essay #3 (North + West)               → builds waitlist for Letters to a Woman
```

One essay per 2–3 week cycle. Never more. Each essay should be a small
event. Treat publishing like releasing a song, not like maintaining a
content channel.

---

## What NOT to write

- ❌ "5 lessons I learned shipping a children's book"
- ❌ "Why I left FastSpring" — billing drama is interesting to founders, not to readers
- ❌ Anything that lists more than three things
- ❌ Anything with a screenshot of a Stripe / Gumroad dashboard in the body
- ❌ Anything with a "Buy now" button at the end. Single text link only.

---

## What every essay must do

- [ ] Open inside a felt moment, not a thesis.
- [ ] Earn the reader's attention with the first paragraph, no headline-cliff.
- [ ] Make exactly one observation, supported by exactly one example.
- [ ] End with a single line link, no urgency, no CTA framing.
- [ ] Survive being read aloud at 11pm.
